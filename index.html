So the workbook SAQ structure — define → describe mechanism & effect → compare/contrast → key difference — is the highest-value thing to rehearse. The SAQ Blueprint tab drills exactly that shape.

How to structure a 10-mark answer

  • Write the headings before the content. Read the question, then jot every required heading first (“Mechanism”, “Clinical uses”, “Side effects” — or each receptor name, then “Key difference”). This stops you dropping a sub-part under time pressure, and shows your logic even if you blank on one section.
  • Allocate the marks as you read. His worked example on the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase question: mechanism ≈3 marks, consequences ≈2, two beneficial effects ≈2–3, one risk ≈2. Do this maths before writing so you spend time proportionally.
  • Always finish a compare/contrast with an explicit “Key difference” line. He named this as the part students most often forget — and it carries marks on its own.
  • Keep it short. “For a 10 mark question, this is about as many words as I would use. I wouldn't go much more than that.” He models dense dot-points, a few sentences per sub-part. Not essays.
  • Attempt every labelled part. Marks are awarded per section — a wrong sentence under a correct heading loses nothing, but a blank heading scores zero.

Wording that decides marks

  • “Risk” ≠ “side effect.” If it asks for a risk, answer with a drug-characteristic issue (narrow therapeutic index, toxicity/accumulation potential). If it asks for a side effect or adverse effect, answer with clinical symptoms. He said the question always uses the precise word deliberately — match your answer type to it.
  • Name the receptor in every mechanism. “Activates FP receptors” earns the mark; “increases outflow” alone does not. State the receptor, then the pathway, then the result.
  • Command words set the depth. Define = one line. Describe / compare / outline = mechanism + effect + example.

From your marked quizzes

  • “Include broader physiological effects for full marks” — the comment that cost you 2 marks on the α/β essay. For every receptor give all five: (1) G-protein, (2) second messenger, (3) cellular effect, (4) organ-level effect, (5) a drug example.
  • “Provide two clearly distinct differences” — you lost a mark restating one idea twice. Number them (1)… (2)… so the marker can't miss them.
  • β1 vs β2 cost you a mark. β1 = heart (“1 heart”). β2 = lungs & vessels (“2 lungs”).
  • On MCQs, when two options are mirror images (increases/decreases GI motility), confirm which branch of the ANS you're in before choosing.
  • The tutorials deliberately ask the same concept several ways — repeating yourself across questions is expected, not a mistake.

Trip-points he flagged himself

Four facts your tutor either paused on during review or was asked to clarify. Highest risk-to-effort ratio in the whole unit.

01

Steady state ≈ 4–5 half-lives. A 10-hour half-life means steady state at roughly 40–50 hours. He called it “one of those things you just have to memorize.”

02

Sublingual is the most effective route for avoiding first-pass metabolism — absorbed straight into systemic circulation via oral mucosa. Rectal only partially avoids it; IM is “less effective”.

03

Ligand vs agonist. A ligand is typically endogenous (insulin, acetylcholine, cortisol). An agonist is often the exogenous drug that mimics it at the same receptor.

04

A partial agonist has high affinity and low efficacy — binds strongly but can't reach maximal response even at full occupancy. Distinct from a competitive antagonist, which has affinity and zero efficacy.
